Become a better rider by thinking like a horse

25 years of listening to horses.

The idea for More Than Human Horsemanship started decades before it was ever established. On a rural farm in Virginia, Ramsey started riding horses before she could walk. She learned natural horsemanship from her mother, and helped train horses under her mother’s guidance from a young age.

The approach was simple: no competitions, just you and your horse.

As Ramsey got older, she began traveling and riding around the world, and even entered her first competition as a mounted archer. She noticed that across countries, cultures, and disciplines, there were many great riders, but too often they rode asking what do I want my horse to do? — instead of asking what is my horse thinking?

Ramsey set out to change that, founding More Than Human Horsemanship with the goal of sharing what her mother and her childhood horses taught her: how to view the world through the eyes of a horse.

To master horsemanship is to master oneself.

Recognizing and analyzing our own thinking, patterns, and biases is a skill that every horseman and horsewoman must dedicate themselves to developing throughout their lifetime.

Our horsemanship is, after all, a reflection of our own awareness and a willingness to step outside of our experiences as humans to be better listeners and partners to our horses.

What we teach

Horse language and psychology

Horses may not speak in words, but they do speak with body language and movement — a language we call Equus.

Equus evolved across millions of years, helping horses survive into the modern day as their ancestors spread from the plains of North America, across the Bering Strait, and into the endless Eurasian Steppe. For these prey animals, silent, subtle communication and keen senses were key to the survival of the herd and the individual horse.

As we learn Equus and horse psychology, we develop the skill some call “horse whispering.” But this isn’t the mystical practice you might think it is. It’s seeing the world through the eyes of a horse and asking yourself: How can I speak Equus too?

Human perspective and communication

You already know what you want to do — that’s easy. But how do we get the horse to willingly do it for you?

Humans and horses have worked together for thousands of years. By studying how horsemanship has varied across time and cultures, we can see how humans have both helped and harmed horses. This shared history with horses leads to a question: How do we communicate? And where have our biases as humans made us accidentally communicate poorly with our horses?

As we look closely at human-horse communication, we see a mirror of ourselves — for better and worse. But we can use that reflection to grow, to change how we interact with our horses, and to speak to them in a way they can more easily understand.

Developing a safe and reciprocal partnership

We cannot change the past, but we can create a safe and calm environment for horses to learn and grow alongside us.

One honest conversation at a time, trust can grow — and so can our relationships with our horses. If we dedicate ourselves to self-reflection and give our horses space to communicate, we can foster a partnership where reciprocal respect grows into horse and human both choosing each other, without force.

We leave the barn door wide open for growth, improved communication, and a willing partner when we center the question: What is my horse thinking?
